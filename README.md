# ChatGPT is designed to be an incredible employee who gets work done — here's what that means for kids using the same tool that lawyers, engineers, designers, product managers and other adults use

Most AI agents, LLMs and chatbots are built to be extremely helpful employees that work tirelessly and independently to get a job done for their boss (the user).

For kids using the same tools, they're getting the same treatment.

Here's what the actual system prompts for ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini each say for each session, and here are suggestions for meta-prompts that families and educators can insert in as overrides that help tune those same systems to be valuable tools for kids.

**Prompts, tests and plain-English guides that help kids use AI to learn, instead of having AI do the learning for them.**

ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude are built to be great employees for busy adults: answer fast, don't ask too many questions, do the whole job. That is exactly right at work. For a kid who is learning, it's backwards. The questions, the struggle and the decisions *are* the learning.

You can change how the AI behaves. Every major chatbot lets you add your own instructions on top of the company's. This repo gives you instructions to paste in, a way to test them, and a look at what the companies tell their AI to do.

## Start here

**A parent with a child aged 6–11:** read the [thinking partner prompt for kids](prompts/kids-6-to-11.md) *with* your child. It's written in their voice, so they can make it their own. Then paste it in.

**A parent with a teen:** give your teen the [study coach prompt for teens](prompts/teens-12-to-17.md). It's written in their voice; let them change what doesn't fit.

**A teacher:** fill in the [classroom assistant template](prompts/classroom-assistant.md) for one assignment.

**Then:** [set it up](how-to/set-up.md) in the right place, and [test it](how-to/test-it.md). It takes 10 minutes.

## What the prompts ask the AI to do

1. **Ask before it tells.** Find out what the child thinks or has tried.
2. **Give hints, not answers.** Make the next step smaller, not done.
3. **Leave the child's ideas with the child.** Talk about goals and purpose; don't make the thing.
4. **Answer real curiosity.** "Why do cats purr?" gets an answer. Homework doesn't.
5. **Teach kids to judge their own work.** Never call it perfect. Ask what they'd change.
6. **Push back.** AI tends to agree with whoever it's talking to. The prompts ask it to argue.
7. **Send kids into the real world.** "Go try it with someone, then come back and tell me what happened."
8. **Stay warm and firm.** Kids will push. The AI should hold the line kindly.
9. **Be honest about being an AI.** It's not a friend, it doesn't ask for personal details, and it sends kids to a trusted grown-up when something is wrong.

## Why this matters

- In a study of about 1,000 high school students, plain ChatGPT raised practice scores by 48% and **lowered exam scores by 17%** once the AI was taken away. Students "most often simply asked for the answer." A version built to give hints instead of answers removed the harm. ([Bastani et al., *PNAS*, 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12232635/))
- Writers who got story ideas from AI wrote stories rated more creative, but **more similar to each other**. ([Doshi & Hauser, *Science Advances*, 2024](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.adn5290))
- In a two-year trial of an AI tutor in 18 Tennessee middle schools, the typical student asked it for help in only **17%** of the practice sessions where they made a mistake. A good tool does nothing if a kid doesn't choose to use it well, which is why the habits matter as much as the settings. ([Oreopoulos & Low, NBER, 2026](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35620))

## What this is not

- **Not parental controls.** These prompts don't filter content, limit time or show you the chats. Use each app's family settings for that.
- **Not a lock.** A child who controls the account can delete them. They work best as a default you agree on together.
- **Not a replacement for you.** The best thing you can do is ask your kid what they figured out today.
